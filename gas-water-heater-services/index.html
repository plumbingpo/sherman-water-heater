No hot water from a gas heater usually means one thing: the burner is not firing, and something told it not to. Gas heaters are built around safety. The control valve will not open unless a thermocouple or flame sensor proves there is a flame, and many models lock out if the flame arrestor screen at the bottom gets clogged or the heater senses spilled exhaust. So the real question is which safety did its job, and why. Across Sherman, TX, the answer is most often a worn thermocouple, a dirty pilot or burner, a lint-choked air intake, a failing gas valve, or a vent that is no longer drafting properly.

Every gas water heater call starts with safety and then moves to testing. If there is a gas odor, leave the home first, call the gas utility from outside, and do not operate switches or try to relight anything. Once the area is safe, our Sherman technicians work through the heater systematically: millivolt readings on the thermocouple, a close look at the pilot and main burner flames, gas pressure at the control valve, a draft test to make sure exhaust is not spilling back, and a CO check around the appliance. Scorch marks at the burner access, rust flakes on the burner, and a lazy yellow flame each point to a different problem, and we chase down the right one.

The fix is usually straightforward once the cause is known. A new thermocouple or flame sensor, a cleaned pilot tube and burner, a cleared flame arrestor screen, or a replacement gas control valve gets most heaters running again the same day. Vent problems, like a disconnected connector or a flue pipe without enough rise, are corrected on the spot. If sediment is rumbling in the tank, we flush it, and if the anode rod is spent, we can replace it. When replacement is the smarter move, we install new gas heaters sized to your household's first-hour demand, with proper atmospheric, power vent, or direct vent venting, a drip leg on the gas supply, and the expansion tank, drain pan, and strapping your local code calls for. When to Call for Gas Water Heater Services

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you money and keep a slow drip from turning into a flooded utility room. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • A rotten-egg odor near the water heater, in which case get out and call the gas company first
  • Leaks at the drain valve, the fittings, or the bottom of the tank
  • Signs of backdrafting, such as soot, condensation, or an exhaust smell at the vent
  • Less hot water than the tank should deliver
  • Soot or scorch marks around the burner access door
  • Loud rumbling from the bottom of the tank
  • No hot water at all, with the burner never firing
  • Pilot outages that keep coming back after a relight

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Two things make gas water heater work a poor DIY project: the parts involve fuel and combustion, and the symptoms overlap. A pilot outage might come from a worn thermocouple, a partly blocked pilot tube, lint on the flame arrestor screen, a downdraft, or an internal valve failure. A wrong guess there just wastes money. Guess wrong on a gas fitting or vent connection and the stakes are much higher, since a small leak or a spillage problem can put gas or carbon monoxide into the living space. Gas valve and heater replacements also typically call for a permit.

Professional service gets you a correct answer and a safe appliance. Instead of trial and error, we confirm thermocouple output, gas pressure, burner flame, and draft with proper tools. Every gas connection we make is tested for leaks before we leave, and vent connectors are secured with the right slope and clearances. When a replacement is needed, we size the new heater by first-hour rating, handle the permit where one is required, and add the expansion tank, sediment trap, drain pan, or seismic straps your home needs. Prevention Tips That Actually Help

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Here are our recommendations:

  • Set the gas valve near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
  • Flush a few gallons from the drain valve periodically to control sediment
  • Install carbon monoxide alarms on every level of the home and test them monthly
  • Keep the area around the heater clear, especially of gasoline, paint, and solvents
  • Watch for a loose, rusted, or sagging vent pipe above the heater
  • Replace a spent anode rod before the tank itself starts to rust
  • Clean dust from around the base so the burner gets plenty of combustion air
