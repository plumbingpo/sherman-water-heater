Every tank water heater has a limited life, and most reach the end somewhere between year eight and year twelve. How long yours lasts in Sherman comes down to water hardness, water pressure, and whether the tank was ever flushed or its anode rod replaced. The warning signs are familiar: hot water with a rusty tint, the pop and rumble of sediment, shorter and shorter showers, and, most urgently, water around the base of the tank. That last one settles the question. When the inner tank rusts through, the leak cannot be patched, and the only real fix is a new heater.

Choosing the replacement is where most of the value is. Matching the old size is not always right. Households grow, bathrooms get added, and a 40-gallon tank that once kept up may now run dry every morning. Tanks get sized by first-hour rating, tankless units by gallons per minute at winter temperature rise, and we explain what each option really costs to buy and to run. A like-for-like gas or electric tank is usually the simplest and least expensive swap. Tankless and heat pump models carry a higher price but can trim operating costs, and they come with their own requirements for gas line size, venting, electrical capacity, or room to breathe. You get the facts, then you decide.

The final stretch is where a careful installer and a rushed one look nothing alike. Our technicians remove and haul away the old tank, install the new heater with new connectors and a proper shutoff, and leak-test every gas and water joint. Venting is sized and sloped to spec, a sediment trap goes on the gas line, and the relief valve gets a discharge pipe that ends where it should. Code items like expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps are added where required, and the permit is handled when your jurisdiction needs one. Most tank replacements are finished the same day. Warning Signs to Watch For

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • Repeated service calls on the same aging heater
  • A household that has outgrown the tank's capacity
  • Energy bills rising while hot water gets harder to come by
  • Loud rumbling or popping that flushing no longer fixes
  • A heater older than most tanks last, usually eight to twelve years
  • Water leaking from the bottom or body of the tank
  • Rust or corrosion on the tank, fittings, or vent connector
  • Rusty or brown water from the hot side only

Simple Ways to Extend Your Water Heater's Life

A few simple habits, like an annual flush and a quick look at the relief valve, can add years to a tank. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Heat pump units need a clean filter and open airflow to run efficiently
  • Start a flushing routine in the first year, before sediment hardens
  • Set the thermostat around 120 degrees to prevent scalding and reduce wear
  • A fresh anode rod at the right time is the cheapest way to extend tank life
  • Write the install date on the tank so you know when to plan the next replacement
  • Make sure an expansion tank is in place if your home has a closed plumbing system
  • Tankless units need a yearly descaling, especially where the water is hard
